The Week in Launch

Vulcan's failure traced to its fuel feed; a quiet week at the pads

United Launch Alliance (ULA) said Wednesday that Sunday's Vulcan failure points to "inadequate fuel supply from the vehicle to the BE-4 engines, not from the performance of the engines," a finding that matters to Blue Origin, which builds those engines, as much as to ULA (Spaceflight Now). Vulcan stays grounded with no return-to-flight date, and 38 Amazon launches and a stack of national security missions wait on it.

Only one orbital launch flew worldwide since Monday's issue: a Chinese Long March 2D carrying the Shijian 24 satellite on Wednesday evening (Xinhua). That brings 2026 to 267 orbital launch attempts and 259 successes, counting GCAT through Sunday plus Wednesday's launch. The pace picks up now: a classified Falcon 9 is set for late Thursday night, an Ariane 64 carries 36 Amazon Leo satellites on Monday, and the rocket for China's Shenzhou 24 crew is on the pad.

I · Recent Launches & Returns

Shijian 24 reaches orbit; Vulcan's first findings

One launch since Monday, a capsule home from orbit, and ULA's first account of what went wrong on Sunday.

Long March 2D: Shijian 24

A Long March 2D lifted off from the Taiyuan Satellite Launch Center in Shanxi province at 5:51 p.m. EST Wednesday (6:51 a.m. Thursday, Beijing time) and put the Shijian 24 satellite into its planned orbit, Xinhua reported, calling it the 678th flight of the Long March rocket family. Jonathan McDowell logged liftoff at 22:51 Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), and the China 'N Asia Spaceflight account identified the rocket as Long March 2D Y106. China has not described the satellite's mission; Launch Library 2 lists it as an experimental satellite bound for a Sun-synchronous orbit (SSO). Warning notices ahead of the flight pointed to a southerly trajectory, and the two-stage rocket, built by the Shanghai Academy of Spaceflight Technology, needs no strap-on boosters (NASASpaceflight).

Vulcan: low thrust from the start

"While we appear to have lost telemetry and the mission at approximately 10 minutes into flight, early indications are that just after liftoff, the launch vehicle experienced low booster performance, resulting in stage separation occurring when the vehicle was low in altitude and below minimal velocity to reach orbit," ULA said Tuesday (Ars Technica). The Centaur V upper stage and the six solid rocket boosters performed normally; the boosters, whose nozzles were redesigned after problems on two earlier flights, burned out and fell away about 1 minute and 35 seconds after liftoff (Spaceflight Now).

ULA's update at 11 a.m. EST Wednesday narrowed it further: "While work remains to determine cause, preliminary indications are that the low booster thrust was the result of inadequate fuel supply from the vehicle to the BE-4 engines, not from the performance of the engines." The company did not say which part of the vehicle fell short or why. ULA President and CEO Mark Peller said Tuesday that the Centaur separated and lit its RL10 engines, but the first stage left it "in such a low-energy state" that it "couldn't make up for that performance deficit" (Spaceflight Now). Engineers are examining whether one of the two BE-4s dropped to lower thrust shortly after liftoff and never throttled back up; tracking video appeared to show one engine slightly dimmer than the other after the boosters were jettisoned (Ars Technica). The BE-4s "worked ok (good news for New Glenn), as did the Centaur CV-L," McDowell wrote.

Varda W-9 lands in South Australia

Varda Space Industries' W-9 capsule landed at the Koonibba Test Range in South Australia at 8:56 a.m. EST Tuesday after 48 days in orbit, the company announced. W-9 and W-8 launched together on Oct. 1, the first two commercial reentry vehicles flown on the same rocket, and W-8 came home on Nov. 5. W-9 carried a government-funded system to capture data during hypersonic flight, and its nose tile held samples of new heat-shield composites. "Varda's W-9 microgravity sat has landed in Australia," McDowell posted.

What's flying

Ariane 64. Arianespace set a window of 6:09 to 6:38 a.m. Monday for VA271, Ariane 6's 10th flight and the company's fourth for Amazon Leo, flying in its most powerful configuration with four P160C solid boosters (Arianespace).

Shenzhou 24. The Long March 2F and Shenzhou 24 spacecraft rolled out to the pad on Tuesday for a launch targeted for about 7:15 p.m. EST November 24 (00:15 UTC November 25), with a full dress rehearsal still to come, China 'N Asia Spaceflight reported. It will be China's first mission with a foreign astronaut, from Pakistan.

Cygnus NG-25. NASA, Northrop Grumman and SpaceX are targeting 10:47 a.m. November 29 for the Cygnus XL, named the S.S. James "Jim" A. Lovell, with about 11,000 pounds of cargo. Jessica Watkins and Luke Delaney of NASA are to capture it with Canadarm2 on December 1, and it will stay until May 2027 (NASA).

IV · Reentries & ISS Traffic

A cargo Dragon waits out the weather

Splashdown weather holds a Dragon at the station, a Cygnus and a Progress are next in line, and ten catalogued objects have come down.

Dragon CRS-35: packed and waiting

"Now targeting no earlier than Friday, November 20 for Dragon's departure from the @space_Station. Teams are continuing to monitor weather at the splashdown locations," SpaceX posted at 4:48 p.m. Wednesday. The cargo Dragon, flying SpaceX's 35th commercial resupply mission for NASA, arrived on Oct. 15 and will splash down in the Pacific off California (NASA).

The Expedition 75 crew kept loading it on Wednesday with samples of 3D-bioprinted blood vessels and antibiotic-resistant bacteria, a circadian lighting system for evaluation and a spacesuit for refurbishment. Joshua Kutryk of the Canadian Space Agency prepared protein crystals for a Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency drug-development study (NASA). NASA's photos also show Watkins packing a failed Canadarm2 wrist joint for the trip home (NASA).

Traffic ahead

Progress MS-36 is scheduled to launch to the station at 12:03 a.m. November 24 (Launch Library 2), and Cygnus NG-25 follows on November 29, with capture on December 1 (NASA). NASA's prelaunch teleconference for the Cygnus is set for noon on November 23.

V · Industry & Regulation

Vulcan's grounding leaves Amazon and the Pentagon short

ULA's customers wait, Blue Origin lands a Google launch, ICEYE bids for Vega's builder, and Rocket Lab ramps up Neutron.

ULA: who waits on Vulcan

Vulcan is grounded again with no return-to-flight date, leaving the Space Force and Amazon, which bought 38 Vulcan launches for its Leo network, waiting. The FAA will oversee the ULA-led investigation, be involved in every step and approve the final report, including any corrective actions. Had Sunday's launch succeeded, Amazon would have launched 436 Leo satellites; with both Vulcan and New Glenn grounded, its other booked rides are Ariane 6 and Falcon 9 (Spaceflight Now). "We truly understand the impact this has on our customers and are committed to returning to flight as safely and quickly as we can," ULA said Tuesday.

Falcon 9, Falcon Heavy and Vulcan are the only rockets that have flown the Pentagon's most demanding National Security Space Launch missions, and in October the Space Force reassigned six of them to SpaceX during Vulcan's earlier grounding (Payload). The setback is likely to sideline Vulcan for at least half a year, and Amazon had more than a dozen Vulcan launches on its 2027 manifest (Ars Technica).

Blue Origin: New Glenn's return, and Google

Blue Origin is targeting January for New Glenn's return to flight after its May pad explosion, according to a memo from chief executive Dave Limp, and a Federal Communications Commission (FCC) filing lists a January 15 to April 15 window (Spaceflight Now). A modified BE-4 main oxygen valve has completed qualification testing; "we are now rolling it out across the remaining fleet," the company said (Ars Technica). On Thursday, Blue Origin and Google announced a launch agreement for Project Suncatcher: New Glenn will fly the project's first heavy-lift launch, targeted for the first quarter of 2029, putting satellites "each with roughly the capacity of a data center rack" into a Sun-synchronous orbit (Google).

Europe: ICEYE bids for Avio

Finnish radar-satellite operator ICEYE launched a €2.05 billion all-cash takeover bid on Tuesday for Avio, the Italian builder of the Vega C rocket, offering €41 a share, a 21.3% premium, and pledging another €1 billion of investment in Italy over five years (European Spaceflight). The deal needs clearances in Italy, France, the United Kingdom and the United States. "Avio has built the launch and propulsion capability Europe relies on," said ICEYE chief executive Rafal Modrzewski. Avio's board is considering the offer; Vega C has flown successfully seven times in eight attempts since 2022 (Ars Technica).

Quick Hit

ClearSpace-1. The European Space Agency (ESA) awarded OHB a €102 million contract on Tuesday to build the spacecraft that will capture and remove ESA's Proba-1 satellite, now expected to launch in 2030 (European Spaceflight).

PLD Space: a Japanese customer

Mitsubishi Electric, which led PLD Space's Series C round, signed on Wednesday to fly a demonstration satellite on the second MIURA 5 test flight. The Spanish rocket's first flight from French Guiana slipped in October to the first half of 2027 (European Spaceflight).

Rocket Lab: Neutron hardware at Wallops

Neutron's second stage and thrust module have arrived at Launch Complex 3 at Wallops Island, Virginia, for fluid tests and launch-mount checks ahead of a debut no earlier than 2027. Rocket Lab signed eight more Neutron missions in the third quarter, lifting its backlog to a record $3.57 billion (NASASpaceflight). Its Long Beach engine center is set up to build and ship "one Archimedes engine every 8 days," the company said Wednesday.

Stoke Space: a rideshare buyer

SEOPS signed a multi-launch deal for rideshare capacity on Stoke Space's Nova Block 2 from as early as 2029, as SpaceX prepares to wind down Falcon 9 rideshare programs around that year. Nova's Pathfinder vehicle is expected to make its first flight early next year (Payload).

Regulation: deadlines ahead

The FAA's virtual scoping meetings on SpaceX's Starbase expansion ran Tuesday through Thursday; comments close December 7 (FAA). Comments on the FCC's space-licensing overhaul (SB Docket No. 25-306) are due November 25 (Federal Register), and on the FAA's proposed exception for failures ending in an uncontrolled atmospheric return (FAA-2026-9942) by December 9 (Federal Register).

The count

Jonathan McDowell's General Catalog of Artificial Space Objects (GCAT) now records 266 orbital launch attempts in 2026 through Sunday's Vulcan; with Wednesday's Long March 2D from Launch Library 2, the year stands at 267, 259 of them successful. SpaceX leads with 126, followed by China with 82, Rocket Lab with 19 and Russia with 15; other U.S. providers have flown 10, Europe seven, India four, Japan three and South Korea one.

The failures

Eight launches have failed this year: India's PSLV on January 12; China's Long March 3B on January 16, Ceres-2 on January 17, Tianlong-3 on April 3 and Long March 7A on August 10; Japan's Kairos on March 5; Blue Origin's New Glenn on April 19, recorded as a partial failure; and ULA's Vulcan on November 15 (GCAT).
