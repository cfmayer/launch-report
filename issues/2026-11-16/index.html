The Week in Launch

A Starship comes home from orbit; Vulcan is lost

Four orbital launches flew worldwide since Thursday's issue, and three reached orbit. On Saturday afternoon the tower at Starbase caught Ship 42 after 22 and a half hours and 14 orbits, the first time SpaceX has caught a Starship upper stage, though Booster 22 waved off its own catch and went into the Gulf (NASASpaceflight). Rocket Lab flew its 100th Electron early Saturday, and Russia launched a Soyuz rideshare from Vostochny on Friday night.

On Sunday, United Launch Alliance's (ULA) Vulcan failed about 10 minutes into its first flight in nine months, losing 40 Amazon Leo satellites. It was the company's first outright failure since it was formed in 2006 (Spaceflight Now). That brings 2026 to 266 orbital launch attempts and 258 successes, counting GCAT through Saturday and the Vulcan. Two classified Falcon 9 missions from Vandenberg have slipped, and the next crewed launch is China's Shenzhou 24.

I · Recent Launches & Returns

Ship 42 caught, Electron at 100, Vulcan down

Four launches between Friday evening and Sunday afternoon: Starship, Soyuz and Electron succeeded; Vulcan did not reach orbit.

Starship Flight 15: a ship catch, a booster in the Gulf

Starship lifted off from Orbital Launch Pad 2 at Starbase at 5:47 p.m. EST Friday (22:47:36 UTC), Jonathan McDowell reported. "Super Heavy has diverted to an offshore splashdown in the Gulf," SpaceX posted minutes later. Booster 22 relit 32 of its 33 Raptors for the boostback burn, but the burn ended early on low fuel mass; its guidance system then triggered an automated abort, and the booster hovered over the water before its flight termination system fired as the engines shut down (NASASpaceflight). "Early indications are that the booster ended its boostback [burn] early due to a low fuel mass alarm," SpaceX's Kate Tice said Saturday; the root cause is still under investigation (Space.com).

Ship 42 went on to orbit. A single-engine, 18-second insertion burn at T+26:06 raised it to a near-circular orbit at 276 kilometers (McDowell), and "all 26 Starlink V3 satellites are in orbit," SpaceX posted at 7:06 p.m. SpaceX made contact with every satellite, and all have begun raising their orbits (Payload).

After a day of coasting, the ship fired one Raptor to leave orbit, crossed Mexico, flew a final "swoosh" over the Gulf and lit three engines for the landing flip (NASASpaceflight). "Mechazilla catches Starship!" SpaceX posted at 4:21 p.m. Saturday. McDowell timed the landing at 4:20:02 p.m. EST (21:20:02 UTC) after a 22-hour, 32-minute flight and called it "the first recovery of an orbital upper stage since Shuttle."

Soyuz-2.1b: a Vostochny rideshare

A Soyuz-2.1b with a Fregat upper stage lifted off from Vostochny at 8:16 p.m. EST Friday (01:16 UTC Saturday) carrying the KOEN and Kinosputnik commercial imaging satellites "and a set of so-far-unidentified cubesats," McDowell noted. Launch Library 2 lists the payloads as dual-use optical Earth-observation satellites and two NPO Kaisant cubesats carrying Germes and Gonets modems. CelesTrak catalogs 13 objects from the launch, 12 of them at about 499 by 511 to 515 kilometers, inclined 97.5 degrees.

Electron's 100th launch

"LIFT-OFF for the 100th time!" Rocket Lab posted as Electron left Launch Complex 1 on New Zealand's Mahia Peninsula at 12:30 a.m. EST Saturday (05:30 UTC) on "The Mountain God Rises." Payload deployment from the kick stage was confirmed about 50 minutes later (Rocket Lab). The radar satellite, iQPS's 11th on Electron, appears in CelesTrak's catalog as QPS-SAR-19 (Yamatsumi-II) at about 568 by 583 kilometers, inclined 42 degrees. Electron reached 100 launches "faster than any commercially developed rocket in history," the company said.

Vulcan: lost during the climb to orbit

Vulcan, in its six-booster VC6L configuration, lifted off from Space Launch Complex 41 at Cape Canaveral at 12:40 p.m. EST Sunday (17:40:30 UTC) after a hold at T-minus 20 seconds, at the end of its launch window (Spaceflight Now). The solid boosters burned out and fell away normally. "Based on data received by our Launch Director, we confirm that the Vulcan vehicle launched from Space Launch Complex 41 suffered an anomaly 10 minutes into the flight and failed to deliver the Amazon mission to orbit," ULA posted at 1:30 p.m. "Since the anomaly just occurred, we don't have any specific data yet to understand what occurred today."

The first stage's two BE-4 engines appeared to fire longer than planned, and the telemetry-driven animation showed the Centaur V upper stage making unusual orientation adjustments early in its burn (Spaceflight Now). Multiple sources told Ars Technica the problem was in the booster stage, which kept firing for at least 30 seconds past its planned shutdown just before the five-minute mark; the animation showed the rocket starting to spin moments after engine shutdown. McDowell flagged the conflict between that account and ULA's T+10-minute timing.

Flight 15's orbit and return

After its ascent burn, Ship 42 was on "a planned suborbital trajectory" while teams checked its systems (SpaceX); its single-engine insertion burn of about 92 meters per second at T+26:06 then raised its perigee to a near-circular orbit (McDowell). The 26 Starlink satellites it deployed reported an orbit of 262 by 272 kilometers inclined 30.5 degrees (McDowell). The flight plan carried three checkpoints where SpaceX could end the mission early, the first bringing the ship down north of Hawaii and the second off Chile (NASASpaceflight). Neither was used. The ship reentered over the Pacific and made a supersonic flight across Mexico and the southwestern United States before turning out over the Gulf to line up for the catch at Pad 2 (Spaceflight Now).

Vulcan's planned profile

Vulcan flew northeast from Cape Canaveral. Two Centaur burns were to carry the 40 Amazon Leo satellites about 285 miles up, where they would separate in 16 events starting about 70 minutes after liftoff (Spaceflight Now). McDowell logged a "FAILURE to reach orbit during first Centaur burn."

IV · Reentries & ISS Traffic

A stage comes back whole; a Dragon readies to leave

Ship 42 is back at Starbase, the station crew loads a cargo Dragon for its return, and nine more catalogued objects have left orbit.

Ship 42 after its return

Within about 20 minutes of the catch, teams had positioned the ship on the tower arms and attached the Ship Quick Disconnect, and its heat shield appeared to be in good condition (NASASpaceflight). Elon Musk (@elonmusk) inspected it on Sunday. "Overall, it looks great. The engines look great too. Ship 42 could probably be reflown with a few weeks of refurbishment," he wrote at 5:20 p.m. Sunday. "However, many small fixes are needed to achieve immediate reusability (like an airplane), which requires zero expected refurbishment, apart from scheduled maintenance."

Booster 22's remains came down in the Gulf. "SpaceX recovery teams were immediately onsite to safe the area," and an environmental health and safety team is recovering any debris that washes onshore, SpaceX posted Saturday morning.

Station: Dragon packed for home

The Expedition 75 crew spent Thursday and Friday loading a SpaceX cargo Dragon, which arrived on Oct. 15 with more than 6,300 pounds of supplies, for its coming departure and splashdown in the Pacific, NASA said. Return cargo includes bacterial DNA, 3D-printed blood vessels and a lighting system that recreates natural daylight, tested for more than three years. Anil Menon and Jessica Watkins of NASA packed and secured cargo on Friday, while Luke Delaney harvested thale cress plants in the Kibo module for return to Earth (NASA).

Traffic ahead

Progress MS-36 is scheduled to launch to the station at 12:03 a.m. November 24 and Cygnus NG-25 at 10:47 a.m. November 29 (Launch Library 2). At Tiangong, Shenzhou 24 will carry one of two Pakistani candidates, named in Pakistani press reports as Muhammad Zeeshan Ali and Khurram Daud, as a payload specialist for about a week. He will return on Shenzhou 23, and one Shenzhou 23 astronaut will stay aboard for China's first year-long mission (Space.com).

V · Industry & Regulation

Vulcan's failure hits Amazon and the Space Force

ULA opens an investigation, SpaceX weighs reflying Ship 42, Rocket Lab marks a century, and Starbase hearings start this week.

ULA: what the loss means

Vulcan is on contract to launch roughly half of the U.S. military's national security missions in the next few years, and Sunday's flight was the first of 38 Amazon has booked; the failure is expected to ground the rocket indefinitely, Ars Technica reported. Amazon Leo has 396 satellites in orbit of a planned 3,292 first-generation spacecraft. "There is risk with any launch, and that's especially true for inaugural missions like LV-01," Amazon said. The flight was also the first with redesigned solid-booster nozzles after the Space Force paused national security Vulcan missions in February, and the first BE-4 flight since a New Glenn exploded on its pad in May; Blue Origin had retrofitted Sunday's engines with a fix (Spaceflight Now). NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman wrote that NASA "is available to assist in all matters related to the investigation and remediation effort. Artemis, Moon Base, and the nation need ULA" (Spaceflight Now). "Sorry to hear this. Hope you are back to flight soon," Musk replied to ULA.

SpaceX: after the catch

"We caught a Starship, and now everything changes," SpaceX's Dan Huot said on the landing webcast. SpaceX has caught Super Heavy three times and reflown boosters twice, months apart, but has yet to reuse a ship (Space.com). Musk said Sunday that Starship V4 "will be ~70m long, which is roughly a 40% length increase and allows >60% more propellant load," with nine engines, adding three more Raptor Vacuum engines.

Rocket Lab: Electron at 100

Rocket Lab has built 100 Electrons powered by 999 Rutherford engines, "one less than 1000 because we re-flew a recovered engine on our 40th launch" (Rocket Lab), and has deployed more than 270 payloads (Rocket Lab). "We have achieved a 96% reliability rate across 99 launches," founder Peter Beck said on the company's Nov. 9 earnings call (Space.com). The Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital revealed a $150 million loan to Rocket Lab on Nov. 10 to support Neutron's flight rate, following a $150 million conditional commitment to Stoke Space on Oct. 26 for a second Cape Canaveral pad (Ars Technica).

Quick Hit

Launch deals. Firefly Aerospace agreed to fly up to 10 Alpha launches for Japan's Synspective through 2030, two radar satellites per flight, and Stoke Space signed a multi-launch agreement with Planet Labs without disclosing terms (Ars Technica).

AST SpaceMobile: a rocket of its own

AST SpaceMobile detailed Blacklift, a three-stage, all-composite rocket with a reusable first stage and 20 tons of lift to low Earth orbit, at a Nov. 12 event in New York. It will use seven Firefly Miranda engines on the first stage and Firefly Vira engines above, carry up to five BlueBird satellites per launch, and fly only AST's satellites, with a first flight targeted for late 2028 (SpaceNews). "We're not doing this thing because we want to be in the launch business," chief executive Abel Avellan said, adding that "we cannot get enough launches on time."

Europe: Themis waits

ArianeGroup's late-October Prometheus engine tests at Lampoldshausen were "conducted independently of Themis flight preparations," a spokesperson told European Spaceflight. The reusable booster demonstrator is still on its pad at Esrange in Sweden, and ArianeGroup and SSC Space will decide by the end of November whether to try its first hop this year.

FAA: Starbase scoping meetings this week

The FAA's five virtual scoping meetings on SpaceX's plan for up to 456 Starship launches a year at Starbase run Tuesday through Thursday, at 2:00 to 4:00 p.m. or 6:30 to 8:30 p.m. EST. Comments are due December 7 (FAA). Also open: comments on the Federal Communications Commission's space-licensing overhaul (SB Docket No. 25-306) close November 25 (Federal Register). Comments on the FAA's proposed exception for failures ending in an uncontrolled atmospheric return (FAA-2026-9942) close December 9 (Federal Register).
