The Week in Launch

Vulcan returns and Starship goes for a double catch on Friday

Two orbital launches flew worldwide since Monday's issue, and both reached orbit: a Long March 2D from Jiuquan with the Chinese Academy of Sciences' Pathfinder-1 science satellite on Wednesday morning, and a Falcon 9 from Vandenberg with the classified USSF-627 mission late Wednesday night, two days later than planned. That brings 2026 to 262 orbital launch attempts and 255 successes, counting GCAT through Sunday and the two launches since.

Friday is the big day. United Launch Alliance's (ULA) Vulcan flies for the first time in more than nine months with 40 Amazon Leo satellites at 12:54 p.m., and the FAA's airspace plan now carries Starship Flight 15 at 5:45 p.m., with a first attempt to catch both the booster and the ship (FAA). Rocket Lab's 100th Electron follows early Saturday. South Korea's military rocket demonstration is off again after a diver died working on its launch barge, and NASA's twin ESCAPADE probes are now headed for Mars.

I · Recent Launches & Returns

A science pathfinder for China; a second classified Falcon in six days

One Chinese and one American launch, both successful, between Monday morning and Wednesday night.

Long March 2D: CAS Pathfinder-1

A Long March 2D lifted off at 6:41 a.m. EST Wednesday (11:41 UTC) from Launch Area 94 at Jiuquan with Pathfinder-1, a Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) satellite bound for Sun-synchronous orbit (Launch Library 2). It tests key technologies for the academy's "Origins" space science program of four astronomy missions due by 2030, and carries a gamma-ray telescope pathfinder, a magnetometer, an upper-atmosphere lidar and a space weather monitor. The satellite "carries three subsatellites which will be deployed later," Jonathan McDowell noted; Launch Library 2 says they will fly in formation to demonstrate a low-frequency radio astronomy interferometer.

Falcon 9: USSF-627

"Liftoff!" SpaceX posted as a Falcon 9 left Space Launch Complex 4E at Vandenberg at 11:36 p.m. EST Wednesday (04:36 UTC Thursday) on USSF-627, a classified U.S. Space Force mission listed in Monday's FAA plan as "R-5." First stage B1103, on its seventh flight, landed on the drone ship Of Course I Still Love You (SpaceX). It was the second classified Space Force launch from Vandenberg in six days, after USSF-432 on Friday, and the 120th Falcon 9 launch of 2026, 85 of them for Starlink, Space.com reported. SpaceX has not described the payload; Launch Library 2 notes the rocket's drop zones match earlier Starlink Group 15 flights.

Sunday's SatNet batch catalogued

The nine SatNet satellites from Sunday's Long March 12 now appear in CelesTrak's catalog as Hulianwang Digui 232 to 240, at about 912 by 930 kilometers, inclined 50 degrees; the rocket's upper stage sits in a 791 by 911 kilometer orbit.

Vulcan's path

"The rocket will launch northeastward from Cape Canaveral, heading up the U.S. East Coast to reach a low Earth orbit," ULA posted, citing a 90 percent chance of acceptable weather from the Space Force's Space Launch Delta 45. A launch readiness review with Amazon, the Space Force and the FAA declared the rocket "GO for liftoff Friday at 12:54 p.m. EST" (ULA), and Thursday is "roll day" (ULA).

Flight 15's timeline

Booster 22 is due back for a catch about 6.5 minutes after liftoff. Ship 42 is to deploy 26 Starlink V3 satellites, circle Earth 14 times and return for its own catch about 22 hours later, Space.com reported. A launch at the opening of the window would bring the ship down at Starbase at 4:17 p.m. EST Saturday (Space.com). Three of the Starlink satellites carry cameras to image the ship's heat shield in orbit, as on Flight 14 (NASASpaceflight).

Spectrum's Norwegian window

An aviation notice from Norway's air navigation provider Avinor opens a launch window for Spectrum's third flight from November 26 through February 17, with attempts possible between 3:00 and 4:30 p.m. EST (20:00 to 21:30 UTC) each day, European Spaceflight reported. Isar Aerospace has not announced a launch date.

IV · Reentries & ISS Traffic

ESCAPADE slingshots toward Mars; a cargo Dragon packs for home

Two powered Earth flybys sent NASA's twin probes on their way, the station crew loads Dragon for next week's departure, and seven catalogued objects left orbit.

ESCAPADE's powered flybys

The second ESCAPADE spacecraft, Gold, flew past Earth at about 6 a.m. EST Monday, coming within about 1,050 kilometers, a little more than 48 hours after its twin Blue passed at about 1,600 kilometers; both fired their engines during the flybys, SpaceNews reported. "This is the first time NASA has used a powered Earth flyby to propel a spacecraft toward Mars, and we've now executed this maneuver twice, flawlessly, in just two days," said Asal Naseri, acting associate flight director in NASA's heliophysics division. Rocket Lab, which built both spacecraft, wrote that "Blue and Gold are on their way to Mars!" The pair are due at Mars on Sept. 7 and Sept. 9, 2027 (NASA).

Station: Dragon departure next week

The Expedition 75 crew spent Tuesday preserving microbiology samples that "are returned to Earth aboard a SpaceX Dragon cargo spacecraft next week for analysis," and moving cargo in and out of Dragon ahead of its departure, NASA said. NASA's Anil Menon and the Canadian Space Agency's Joshua Kutryk processed bacteria samples in the Kibo laboratory, while station commander Pyotr Dubrov and Anna Kikina serviced three Russian Orlan spacesuits in the Poisk module. The crew is also clearing space for supplies arriving on Northrop Grumman's Cygnus XL in a few weeks. On the schedule, Cygnus NG-25 is set to launch at 10:49 a.m. November 29, after Progress MS-36 at 12:03 a.m. November 24 and China's Shenzhou 24 to Tiangong NET November 24 (Launch Library 2).

V · Industry & Regulation

Booster 22 rolls out; Neutron's price goes up

SpaceX readies the first double catch, the FAA moves Starbase hearings online, Rocket Lab raises prices on strong demand, and NASA explains its plan for SLS.

SpaceX: Booster 22 on the pad

"Super Heavy moved to the launch pad at Starbase. Booster 22 is the first Super Heavy V3 booster planned to return to the launch site for catch and was used for final catch testing upon arrival, including a suspended squeeze test at catch height," SpaceX posted at 1:21 p.m. EST Tuesday; Elon Musk (@elonmusk) shared it Wednesday. Booster 22 and Ship 42 are both on their first flights, and Flight 15 will be Starship's 15th full-stack launch and the fourth for the V3 version, with the first booster catch attempt since Flight 8 (NASASpaceflight). SpaceX has caught Super Heavy three times but has never tried to catch a ship (Space.com).

FAA: Starbase hearings go online

The FAA has moved all public scoping meetings on SpaceX's plan for up to 456 Starship launches a year at Starbase online, including those set for November 17 and 19, and extended the comment period to December 7 (FAA). Five virtual meetings run November 17 to 19, at 2:00 or 6:30 p.m. EST. Tuesday's Federal Register notice (docket FAA-2026-12808) says later Starship license modifications or renewals fall within the scope of the programmatic environmental impact statement.

ULA: Vulcan's comeback flight

Friday's flight debuts the VC6L, with six solid rocket boosters and the long payload fairing, and a low-Earth-orbit version of the Centaur V upper stage that is 1.94 meters smaller than the High Energy version flown on every earlier Vulcan, NASASpaceflight reported. It is Vulcan's fifth mission, a certification flight of the configuration for the U.S. Space Force, and the first of 38 Vulcan launches scheduled for Amazon Leo. A dedicated processing lane with purpose-built infrastructure for Amazon's constellation "is ready to support its first launch at Cape Canaveral," ULA posted Thursday.

Rocket Lab: Neutron at $70 million

Rocket Lab raised the price of a dedicated Neutron launch from $55 million to $70 million, citing strong demand and a tight launch market, and said it booked eight new Neutron missions in the third quarter worth more than $515 million, doubling the rocket's backlog, Payload reported. Neutron's first launch has slipped to 2027, and the company also expects to raise Electron and HASTE prices. "Electron is all dressed up for launch 100," the company posted Tuesday.

Quick Hit

United Kingdom. The government now holds more than 25 percent, but not more than 50 percent, of SaxaVord Spaceport's operating company after a £60 million public-private investment, according to Companies House filings (European Spaceflight).

NASA: the SLS road map

NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman said industry responses on future commercial lunar transportation point to the 2030s, so the agency will standardize the Space Launch System (SLS), move to a stretched Centaur V upper stage and stop work on Mobile Launcher 2, NASASpaceflight reported. "There are a lot of ways to shake the $4 billion disposable rocket label that SLS has carried and one of which is launch more frequently," he said. Both the last Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage and a stretched Centaur V remain options for Artemis IV.

Europe: MaiaSpace's contract

The European Space Agency (ESA) has not announced signing MaiaSpace's European Launcher Challenge contract because the company alone chose support through launch service contracts, worth €183.4 million, rather than development co-funding; ESA signed €543.6 million in contracts with Isar Aerospace, Rocket Factory Augsburg and PLD Space in August (European Spaceflight).

Still open

Comments on the Federal Communications Commission's space-licensing overhaul (SB Docket No. 25-306) are due November 25 (Federal Register), and comments on the FAA's three latest proposals to ease Part 450, its launch and reentry licensing rule, are due December 9 (Federal Register).

The count

Jonathan McDowell's General Catalog of Artificial Space Objects (GCAT) records 260 orbital launch attempts in 2026 through Sunday; with Wednesday's Long March 2D and Falcon 9 from Launch Library 2, the year stands at 262, 255 of them successful. SpaceX leads with 125, followed by China with 81, Rocket Lab with 18 and Russia with 14; other U.S. providers have flown nine, Europe seven, India four, Japan three and South Korea one.

The failures

According to GCAT, seven launches have failed this year: India's PSLV on January 12; China's Long March 3B on January 16, Ceres-2 on January 17, Tianlong-3 on April 3 and Long March 7A on August 10; Japan's Kairos on March 5; and Blue Origin's New Glenn on April 19, recorded as a partial failure.
